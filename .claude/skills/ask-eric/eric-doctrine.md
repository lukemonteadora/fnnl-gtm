## 1. The four levers, in strict order (the master diagnostic)

When anything underperforms, walk these in order and fix the highest broken one first. Do not touch a lower lever while a higher one is broken.

0. **Deliverability is a precondition, not a lever.** Before blaming any lever, rule out spam. Reply rate near 0.1%, or a sudden jump in contacts-per-reply, means you are in spam. Rotate domains. Never misread a deliverability problem as a bad offer. And never call the offer broken on a tiny sample (under a few hundred sends). **Section 16 covers how he actually maintains this precondition day to day** (reserve capacity, health-check rhythm, domain retirement rules); this bullet is the diagnostic principle, section 16 is the operating system behind it.
1. **Offer** (highest leverage by far). Change this first.
2. **List segmentation.** Get more refined; speak to the shared pain of a smaller, sharper slice.
3. **Personalization.** A multiplier, never a fix. Eric has never once seen personalization save a dead campaign; it only makes a working one better.
4. **Volume.** Last resort, only after the above are dialed and the math (LTV:CAC, TAM size) says more sends will pay.

## 2. Offer (lever 1, the one that matters most)

- **The single trait every excellent campaign shares: the offer is easy to say yes to.** If you fix nothing else, make the ask cheap to accept.
- **SQL vs MQL.** A sales-qualified ask is "we solve X, want to meet Tuesday to solve it?" A marketing-qualified ask is a low-friction hand-raiser ("want this free thing?"). If the direct meeting ask is hard to say yes to right now, drop to an MQL/lead-magnet offer and start the conversation, then convert later. Everyone wants SQLs; not every offer can earn them cold.
- **Chunk up / chunk down.** If the plain offer stalls: chunk up (talk only about the outcome, hide the mechanism; "we help law firms grow revenue," not "we're an SEO agency"), or chunk down (sell one sexy, specific feature). Both are reframes, not new offers.
- **Timing beats persistence on the wrong ask.** The vacuum example: "want to buy vacuums?" to hotels flopped; "who buys these, so we're the next call when you do?" crushed. You cannot know when they want the thing, so position to be there when they do.
- Nod to Hormozi's value equation for building the offer itself (dream outcome, likelihood, time delay, effort).
- **Eric's own "Five Pillars" for a cold-traffic offer specifically** [dated, Jul 2026]: an immediately understood outcome, a clear economic benefit, a mechanism the buyer finds credible, lower perceived risk, and a simple next step or useful free value. He derived it watching which of his own customers stuck around past six months, organized around make-money / save-money-or-time / a new mechanism / proof / risk reduction. This overlaps with but isn't identical to the Hormozi equation above; it's more of a stranger-specific checklist than a general value formula, and it's worth using as a five-line gut check on a draft offer: write one sentence per pillar, cut any claim you can't back, price the promised free work, and test with a handful of target buyers before building a full campaign around it.
- **Real examples he cites: numeral.com** (automate sales-tax collection, an SQL offer that works cold because the pain is big and hairy); **a creator-affiliate connector** (easy-yes because it's pay-on-affiliate); **his own "list of people engaging with your competitors"** (an MQL hand-raiser).
- **A reversed-pitch archetype, worth naming separately** [dated, Sep 2026]: instead of offering the prospect something free or paid, pay *them* for an asset they already hold. Eric authored and endorses the premise of an outreach email offering to buy a company's internal operational data (sales processes, support tickets, SOPs, internal messages) for AI-training use, with headline payout figures quoted inside the copy itself. Flag those specific dollar figures for what they are: marketing claims inside the outreach copy about an unnamed buyer's payouts, relayed through a third party, not Eric's own verified agency numbers, so don't cite them with his usual "it's just my data" disclaimer, since that disclaimer implies a level of first-party grounding these figures don't have. The reusable idea, independent of the unverified numbers, is real: some offers run in the opposite direction of value flow from every other example in this section.

## 3. List and TAM ("the list is the message")

- **Pull the WHOLE TAM up front, programmatically.** Not CSV dumps, not game-time decisions. If the AI picks segments on the fly it chooses thin, low-coverage slices. Build the complete board first so every future experiment slices from it. Building the whole board does not mean sending to it in arbitrary order: his default first cut is the growth filter in section 8, which orders the TAM rather than replacing it.
- **Forecast the email yield.** Only 50 to 70% of contacts produce a validatable email (industry-dependent). A "100k TAM" is really ~60k of runway. Know when the list drains so you expand before you starve. **A dated refinement worth carrying with this number** [Sep 2026]: the effective yield also depends on which email validator you use, since validators disagree on borderline catch-all domains. Eric found one validator flagged roughly 14.6% of a sample of addresses that had actually produced real replies as catch-all (his own reported figure; the exact base sample size is not cleanly established across his own restated versions of it), and a second validator recovered them. A conservative validator silently understates your real yield; see section 15 for the general benchmarking method that catches this.
- **Five list-building methods, matched to the target:**
  1. **LinkedIn databases** (~80% of lists; the default when contacts have LinkedIn profiles). **Caveat added by a dated Jul 2026 benchmark:** this yield figure is implicitly conditional on the ICP actually having a LinkedIn footprint. For businesses weak on LinkedIn (a lot of local/SMB targets), website- and search-based routes can outperform LinkedIn-derived databases outright, which is exactly why method 3 below exists.
  2. **Lookalikes** (when the niche has no clean LinkedIn industry; seed 3 to 5 known-good companies, then curate hard, don't trust the raw looks). Note this method is *also* an S-tier signal in its own right, for a reason that has nothing to do with list-building: see section 8's proof-transfer bullet.
  3. **Google Maps** (local businesses with weak LinkedIn; the hard part is finding the owner/contact). **Concrete mechanic, dated Aug 2026:** enumerate by category × ZIP code through a third-party maps API, deduplicate the results, then research each business's website and search presence for the actual owner name and a contact route. Pilot one category in a few locations first and measure the duplicate rate, active-business rate, and verified-email coverage before scaling; Google Maps Platform's own terms restrict how far you can push bulk reuse of the data, so check current terms before scaling this hard.
  4. **Custom scraping** (directories, associations, government, filings, **conference sponsor and exhibitor lists**; hidden gold, less competition, smaller so scrape several). On events specifically, he uses an agent to find an upcoming industry event and pull its sponsors, then finds contacts at each. Watch the tension with his own "less competition" logic: his demo pulls Dreamforce and SaaStr, which are the most-scraped sponsor lists in B2B software. The principle points at niche and regional shows, not the famous ones.
  5. **AI agents** (ultra-niche, small TAMs you can't find any other way).
- **A sixth, standing method worth naming on its own** [dated, added Aug-Sep 2026]: a self-updating public list of people who just changed roles, refreshed automatically on a roughly daily cycle and filtered down by company, seniority, location, and relevance before use. It's the same new-in-role signal already S-tier in section 8, just packaged as a maintained, reusable list-source rather than a one-off pull; sample a few dozen records for date accuracy before trusting a batch, since titles and profiles lag reality.
- **Orchestrate multiple point-solution providers instead of leaning on one all-in-one database** [dated, Aug 2026]: rather than treating a single database-style tool as the whole list-building layer, define the target companies and seed accounts, expand to similar companies across several specialized search and enrichment providers, then find and verify contacts, with the orchestration logic itself packaged as a reusable, versioned skill rather than a one-off script. This doesn't retire the all-in-one option for teams that specifically want built-in sequencing and activity tracking in one interface; it's presented as a supplement/alternative, not a universal replacement. Before dropping an existing provider for this, benchmark first per section 15.
- **Filter on the specific department's size when that's what the offer actually depends on, not total company headcount** [dated, Sep 2026]: if the buyer only matters at a certain team size (his example: 3 to 100 salespeople), query departmental headcount directly rather than filtering on overall company size, which can badly over- or under-include. This is a different mechanic from the department-*growth* signal in section 8: one is a static snapshot of size, the other is a rate of change over time. They pair well together but answer different questions.
- **Sales Navigator is the source of truth** every other tool resells secondhand; get signals from it first and fast. Underused filters: past-company (case-study campaigns, "you used to work at Coca-Cola, they were our customer"), groups (worst discovery on the internet, which is exactly why membership is a strong signal), new-in-role (filter years-in-company AND years-in-position separately, because a promotion is not a new job). **A dated Sep 2026 clip narrows this claim usefully rather than reversing it:** Sales Navigator is the current reference specifically *for LinkedIn-derived data*, and which non-LinkedIn provider mix is actually best still depends on the niche and on each provider's own rate limits and coverage; his own provider mix reflects that, not a universal prescription. Read "source of truth" as "the right default starting point for LinkedIn-shaped data," not "never benchmark anything else," and see section 15 for how he actually decides between providers when the niche is unusual.
- **Segment by shared pain.** A 2-person agency and a 50-person agency are different companies with different problems. The win from segmentation is not tighter targeting, it is that a smaller list lets you name the exact problem they're living.
- **"The list is the message": who they sell to beats what they are.** Every platform segments on what a company *is* (industry, headcount, tech stack), and that rarely changes what you'd actually write. What changes the message is **who they sell to**. A company selling into legal teams lives in a different world than one selling to restaurant owners: different buyers, different pain, different proof. So their customers are a real axis to cut on (the vertical they sell into, the size of their customers, the department they sell to), and it's the axis that most reliably earns a genuinely different email. It's a targeting axis, though, not a line to write: segment on it and let it pick the buyer, the pain and the proof you reach for, but don't recite it back at them. "I saw you sell to fitness coaches" is the exact dead opener from section 4. Note the tension with his own tech-stack position in section 8: an install is a fine *filter* for how sophisticated they are, it's just a weak reason to write them a different message.
- **The split test: a data point earns its own campaign only when it changes what you'd say.** If it would change the message, that's the reason to split. If it wouldn't, don't split. This is the discipline that stops "segment harder" from degenerating into slicing the TAM on every column you happen to have collected.

## 4. Copy

- **Length: 70 to 90 words is the sweet spot [his data]. Below 70 underperforms** (not enough credibility or value-prop room). The Creative Ideas Campaign is the accepted longer exception; if it runs long, you can compress the whole thing to one sentence.
- **The Creative Ideas Campaign is the flagship, S-tier, best performer across every client type for 2.5 years.** Structure: "Saw your company. If I were in your shoes, here are 3 specific ways I'd use [our thing] to grow," each idea personalized to them. Rules that make it work: (1) each idea is constrained to a capability you can *actually deliver* (a dev shop once had AI invent features it couldn't build; great replies, angry client), (2) give the AI heavy context on your business, (3) hand-write 3 to 4 real examples so it learns your tone and judgment. Can be compressed to one line: "if I were looking at your business, I'd help this way, [AI-generated specifics]." **Still his stated best performer for several clients as of a dated Sep 2026 clip** — don't read the A-tier/S-tier shift below as him having quietly retired this campaign type; as of that date he hadn't.
- **Second value prop in the PS line = 5x more responses [his test].** If the body pitches save-time, the PS pitches save-money: "PS, if that's not interesting, we also help with X."
- **Don't overdo spintax.** Over ~15 variables hurt; ~5 copy streams is fine. (Caveat: he still hammers spintax when deliverability drops, as an SOP.) **A named, dated instance of exactly that SOP exception** [Aug 2026, credited to Maildoso's founder, not to Eric himself]: "super spintax," meaning many alternatives across the subject, body, unsubscribe line, *and* signature at once, not just subject and body. He pairs this with an explicit compliance line worth carrying forward as its own rule, not just a spintax footnote: **never rotate false titles across variants (don't spin "CEO" for someone who isn't), and variation must never weaken or break the opt-out/unsubscribe language.**
- **Don't name unrecognizable case studies.** Naming a company the prospect doesn't recognize correlates with average and below-average campaigns ("I'm not Reddit, so this doesn't fit me"). Describe the outcome generically, or name proof only when it's recognizable to that reader. Make social proof more generic by default.
- **Stop generic AI personalization.** "I saw you sell to fitness coaches and wanted to reach out" got 143% fewer replies [his data]. Generic summarize-what-they-do personalization is dead. Signal-based and creative-ideas personalization is still A-tier. He was a 2024 believer; he killed it mid-2025.
- **The A-tier vs S-tier shift.** He used to chase "an email as good as a human would send after 10 minutes of research" as the ceiling; he now calls that A-tier. S-tier is dropping heavy personalization in favor of an easy-yes value or lead-magnet offer delivered at scale.
- **"Seven Ingredients" for stronger copy, as a diagnostic checklist, not a rewrite mandate** [dated, Aug 2026]: a relevant signal, clear segmentation, specificity, social proof, a real pain, risk reversal, and a favorable effort-or-value asymmetry. His own point in presenting it: diagnose which one or two ingredients are actually missing from a weak draft rather than polishing the wording all over again, and accept that not every offer can use every ingredient. Score a draft 0 to 1 on each, rewrite only the weakest two, then run a single clean variable test rather than a full rewrite.

## 5. Subject lines

- 2 to 4 words max. First name in the subject correlates with excellent campaigns [his data], though he flags it as overused, so test it rather than adopt it blindly. Mismatched case (first letter capital, rest lowercase) and company name both showed lift.
- Lowercase for personalized subjects unless a proper noun needs a capital. No emojis, no exclamation points, no punctuation tricks.
- **Engineer the subject and the preview text together for the phone push notification.** The subject creates an open loop; the preview deepens it. Design for how it renders on a lock screen.
- "Quick question" and "intro" still work as subject lines despite the hate, if paired with strong preview text. Test them against your best ideas. (Note: this is a *subject-line* claim; it does not license "quick question" as a body opener.)
- The gut check: would a colleague or a customer actually send this subject? If it reads like marketing, it fails.
- David Ogilvy frame: the only job of the subject is to earn the open (the first line), whose only job is to earn the next line.
- Example he loves: for a pizza-restaurant client, subject "pizza orders," preview "I tried calling but figured email was easier," whole email about getting more pizza orders. Impossible to ignore, no bait-and-switch.

## 6. Follow-ups

- **Email 1 gets the most positive replies, every time, then it drops off.** Email 3 drops a lot; email 4 is tremendously lower and raises spam-marking risk.
- **Max 2 to 3 emails, ideally 2.** He will die on this hill. Most people should send 2.
- **Sequence length by TAM size:** 1M+ contacts = send 1 email, hit the whole TAM, come back with new insight and hit it again (no follow-ups); 50k to 100k = 2 emails; under 50k = 3 emails.
- **Space 3 to 5 days between touches, then re-blast the whole TAM 1 to 3 months later.** Follow-ups don't fail because you're annoying; they fail because nothing changed in the prospect's business. Time is the variable: situations change, and nobody remembers your email anyway. (His stage retort: "name one cold email you got today." Nobody can.)
- **Flip the angle in email 2** (save-time in E1 becomes make-money in E2) to let them see it a different way.
- **Lower the ask in the follow-up.** E1 goes for the meeting (SQL); E2 drops to a hand-raiser / lead magnet (MQL).
- **"Building it for you" two-step** (credit Kyle Rasmusen). Email says "I came across your company, I'm putting together a report/sample for you," then the follow-up delivers it. Pattern interrupt, raises perceived value, now trivial to do at scale with AI.
- **Name-drop a colleague in the follow-up.** Pull another leader at the company (the CEO or director when you wrote the VP) and ask "is this you, or should I be talking to them?" Reliably surfaces the right contact.

## 7. Personalization (lever 3, a multiplier)

- **The one-two punch: the research AI is a SEPARATE step from the message-writing AI.** Never ask one AI to both research and write; AI is good at one focused job at a time. Research first, then a second call normalizes the research into the sentence a human would actually write. **Confirmed again in a dated Sep 2026 clip**, where an agent-built enrichment graph keeps "enrich the company description" and "draft a compliment line" as two separate sequential steps rather than one blended prompt.
- **Structured vs unstructured data.** Structured data (the new-in-role date on a profile) is identical for everyone, so it's overused and it leaks the signal. Unstructured research (reading their pricing page, inferring their goals) is where 99% of people never go, and it's where the real personalization lives. **Read this as a copy rule, not a targeting rule, or it looks like it contradicts section 8.** New-in-role is S tier to *target* on and still leaky to *mention*. The two live together: filter on it, don't write it down.
- **Unstructured research is worth the tokens only when the insight is actionable, not merely personalized.** He dismisses the product-launch first line ("that's going to cost you tons of tokens to structure this unstructured data, it's just okay") in the same breath as praising unstructured job-description mining. The difference isn't the data type. It's whether what you extracted names a problem you can solve.
- **The ICP / research prompt loop.** Show 10 results, correct them, re-run, until it's clean two rounds in a row; then lock the prompt and run it across the whole list. Don't rewrite the prompt endlessly; add examples until it sees what good looks like.
- **The cost hack.** Use your Claude Code subscription usage (sub-agents) to do ICP filtering and line-writing instead of paying per-token API calls. Big margin lever at scale.
- **Personalization never saves a dead campaign.** If the offer and list are wrong, personalization does nothing. It only multiplies something already working.

## 8. Signals and triggers

- **Signals are overhyped, including by Eric.** Many signals evaporate if you just have a better, easier-yes offer. Don't reach for signals to rescue a weak offer.
- **Discover signals by mining responses, not only by guessing up front.** Route every positive (and negative) reply through full enrichment after the fact and find what's actually true about responders. His example: the real driver was "they have a sales-ops person," not the AE:SDR ratio the client assumed. This tests many signals cheaply, on replies only, instead of paying to enrich the whole TAM to test a hunch. **A second, dated use of the same instinct** [Sep 2026]: your recent positive-reply list is also a ready-made known-good answer key for benchmarking email validators, not just for finding message-driving signals; see section 15.
- **A signal's default job is to pick the list, not to appear in the copy.** This is why signals look overhyped: they work, and then people ruin them by writing them down. "Most often, we actually do not use this signal in our messaging. We will use it as a filter." Three things stay out of the copy. Anything saturated, because the signal is real but every other sender is already saying it (funding is banned outright at his agency: no "congrats on the raise," filter only). Anything that reveals surveillance, because it's creepy and buys you nothing (never tell someone you found them from your website-visitor tool, never say you saw them like a post). And anything that names no problem, because growth, department growth and funding tell you they *can* buy, not what hurts. **A concrete, dated confirmation of the surveillance ban** [Sep 2026]: he sources leads from people who reacted to or commented on relevant LinkedIn posts, then explicitly warns against ever surfacing that engagement in the message itself; the signal decides who gets researched, never what the email says.
- **The exception: say the signal out loud only when it names a specific problem in their world.** A competitor's new landing pages, a live one-star complaint (theirs or a competitor's, all of it sitting public on Google, Trustpilot and G2, and worth saying only if you connect it to solving it), a competitor's price hike, a requirement buried in a job description. Each is about *them*, not about your research. The test is two-part: does it name a problem, and is it specific enough that a hundred other senders aren't writing the same sentence today. Competitor movement is the strongest thing in this bucket, because "everybody loves hearing about what their competitors are doing": a newsletter service got *Anthropic* to reply by naming OpenAI. It still has to clear the test, though. "We have a strategy we don't think [competitor] is using" names a problem and passes. "I saw you follow this company" names none and fails.
- **Hiring signal: the job description is far more valuable than the job post.** The post tells you they're hiring, which everyone can see and everyone emails about. The description tells you what they're actually trying to do: the keywords in the requirements, the activities they want performed, the problems they're admitting to, and any tech they name that you integrate with, compete with, or want to displace. Cheap AI models are what made mining that text at scale newly practical. The banned version is the pitch-swap: "I noticed you're hiring an SDR, you don't have to, use my cold email stuff." The working version quotes the requirement you mined and asks about it: "I saw you need an ABM expert in this new enterprise SDR role. How's the search going for that person? We have a service that does XYZ." Note he *does* name the role there. What gets you written off is naming the bare fact of hiring, not naming the role.
- **Tech stack: displace, integrate, or filter, and the filter use is the sleeper.** Displace (they run your competitor), integrate (you plug into what they already run), or read the install itself as a sophistication score: "a company that has Triple Whale installed is a way different marketing organization than a company that does not." That third use is the one people sleep on. What they've installed tells you how they operate and what they already pay for, so it scores fit before you write a word. Get it from the stack-detection tools, or from the tech named in their job posts. Displace and integrate name a real problem and survive the say-it-out-loud test above; the sophistication read is filter-only. **A concrete, dated two-step method for coverage gaps** [Sep 2026]: check a structured tech-detection provider first, and only when that comes back empty, fall back to an AI-driven inspection of the site's raw HTML for a technology-specific signature. Same "exhaust the cheap/structured route first" shape as the growth filter and the email-finding waterfall elsewhere in this file, applied here to tech-stack detection specifically. Caution that comes with it: a detected script can be installed but unused, so this is still a filter-only signal, not proof of active use.
- **A custom trigger beats every public signal, and it is the only true S tier.** Everything on the common list is a commodity: your competitors buy the same data from the same vendor on the same day. The only signal nobody else has is the one you build off the moment that actually predicts a purchase in your business. A Google-reputation agency fires on a new negative review. A parking-lot resurfacer reads satellite images for cracks and worn paint (credit: Jordan Crawford). The corollary matters as much as the rule: a signal that is C tier for everyone can be S tier for you. Search-visibility gaps are C tier in general and S tier if you're an SEO agency.
- **The growth filter is the first list he pulls, before anything else.** Outbound works on companies trying to grow and does nothing on companies at status quo, so the cheapest quality lever on any TAM is to send to the growing ones first. His actual default: take the client's normal filters, then keep only companies with **1% or more headcount growth in the last 6 months** [his operating default, not a measured threshold: he never claims to have tested 1% against other cut-offs]. Any growth evidence substitutes: department headcount, website traffic, social following moving at all, whether they're posting jobs at all. On a 50,000-contact TAM he starts with the ~5,000 that are growing, because "more growth means more problems to solve." He often doesn't tell the client he's doing it and never mentions it in the email. **A concrete department-level calculation, dated Sep 2026, for the department-growth entry specifically:** measure current department headcount and recent starters in that same department, then normalize the difference by the department's size, rather than reading a raw headcount number. This is a rate calculation (growth over time); it's a distinct mechanic from section 3's static department-*size* filter, and the two pair well but aren't the same question.
- **Lookalike companies are S tier because they carry a case study, not because they're a list-building fallback.** The signal isn't "this company resembles that company." It's that resemblance is what lets your proof transfer. "I ran outbound for clay.com. You are a competitor to clay.com. Wouldn't you want me to run the outbound? This is the result I got." This is also the one construction where naming the case study is safe (section 4's don't-name-unrecognizable-proof rule): a lookalike of Clay, by definition, knows who Clay is.
- **A signal tells you when. Your ICP still decides who.** Score every company a signal hands you, and if you wouldn't have emailed them on a normal cold campaign, don't email them because a tool flagged them. A customer once handed him a de-anonymized website-visitor list and said email all of these. Nobody cleaned it, and they emailed the CEO's wife. People got really mad.
- **Conditional messaging.** Gate with must-haves (headcount, business type), then stack signals for "golden" status; route messaging by which conditions are true. Bake the micro-decisions a rep would make into the data layer so the team does higher-leverage work.
- **The three signals customers beg for that never earn their place.** M&A / private equity: coverage is bad and the outcome forks into two losing branches (the acquirer says "we're bigger, we already solved this," the acquired says "they make all the decisions now"). "I think we could go for the rest of our lives and not use mergers and acquisitions and we would be okay." Cost-cutting and layoffs: "If there was just a layoff, they're not buying anything," and the tempting logic (they cut the sales team so they'll automate sales) is backwards. Product launches: by the time you see it you've missed the boat, since you'd rather reach them before the launch.
- **His 2026 signal tier list, ranked on three criteria.** **Effectiveness:** does finding it actually translate into them needing to buy. **Accessibility:** how easy and how cheap is the data to get. **Scalability:** do enough companies qualify that you could build a pipeline off it. Grade any new signal an operator brings you on those three. He hedged the whole exercise on the way in ("still take this video with a grain of salt"), vibe-coded the list, and revised three tiers live while reading it out, so it is his prior, not a table of law. **S:** custom/proprietary triggers, new-in-role, lookalike companies. **A:** general growth, competitor movements, customer complaints, job-post data, tech-stack, website visitors, social activity. **B:** department growth, marketing-channel intelligence, lawsuits and compliance. **C:** funding, search-visibility gaps, pricing changes. **D:** mergers/acquisitions/PE, cost-cutting and layoffs, product launches. **All 20 of his own listed "reusable playbooks" as of a dated Sep 2026 demo map cleanly onto this existing tier list** (new-in-role, funding, job-post keywords, LinkedIn posts and engagement, lookalikes among them), which is worth citing as fresh, dated confirmation that this tier list is still his live operating list, not a one-off video moment.
- **The one signal the stack can't produce is real-time third-party intent** (Bombora / 6sense / G2). Everything else you can assemble, including the first-party version: website-visitor de-anonymization is A tier and worth running even though the data is mostly junk, because the setup is one-time and the ICP score catches the rest. "Even if you're incorrect and only 10% of what these tools are telling you are people who will land on your website, it is absolutely worth it to set it up." If the data were accurate it would be S tier.

## 9. Metrics (the three North Stars)

Track these three; ignore the rest.

1. **Reply rate.** Deliverability signal plus a read on offer and list at once. Floor of ~1% after 200-plus sends; below ~0.1% you're in spam. **Getting stuck around 0.6 to 0.7% usually means the list isn't as targeted as you think** — read that as a diagnostic floor to push past, not as a hard ceiling on what's achievable. **A dated Aug 2026 campaign of his own went from ~0.41% to ~3.34%** off a bundle of infrastructure and strategy changes made together (reserved inbox capacity, heavier controlled message variation, signature variation, tighter case-study proof among them), and he's explicit that no single tactic in the bundle gets sole credit for the jump. Two honest caveats travel with that number: the metric as reported counted all messages sent rather than a clean unique-lead denominator, so treat the multiplier as directional, not a precise before/after; and he separately warns that concentrating capacity onto an apparently-"winning" segment can contaminate the very comparison used to call it a winner, so a rate this high deserves the same volume-and-comparability scrutiny as any other campaign result.
2. **Contacts-per-positive-response.** The forecasting metric and the early-warning metric. Bands [his data, relative to LTV:CAC and TAM]: Excellent 150 or fewer, Above-average 150 to 400, Average 450 to 800, Below-average 800 to 1,200, Poor above 1,200. A rising ratio (e.g. 1-in-300 becomes 1-in-700 mid-week) is an early spam / list-drift alarm, and a sign you've expanded into worse-fit contacts.
3. **Net-new email-1 sends per day.** Track first-touch volume specifically, not total sends, because most positive replies come from email 1 and sequencers drift toward sending more follow-ups.

**Ignore:** open rates (the tracking pixel now trips a Gmail spam banner and *drops* reply rates, so he removed it entirely), spam-placement tests (unreliable both ways as a campaign-performance read; he's seen them pass inboxes that were clearly in spam and vice versa), platform "inbox reputation" scores (don't trust them), and bounce rate (once the verification process is dialed it sits ~2% and stops being worth watching as a north star). **This "ignore" list is about which numbers to judge a campaign's performance by. It does not mean never run these checks at all** — section 16 covers spam checks, seed tests, and bounce monitoring as ongoing *infrastructure* diagnostics, a different job from reading them as a campaign's report card, and that use is not in tension with this list.

**Benchmarks are relative.** A Google-reputation offer might pull 1-in-70; an enterprise-comms overhaul might pull 1-in-2,000 and still print money because the TAM is huge and the LTV:CAC works. "If the metric changes but your behavior doesn't, you're tracking the wrong metric" (Hormozi).

**Timing:** best reply days are Monday to Thursday, Tuesday to Thursday even better. Drop Friday afternoon. For a very small TAM, send only Tuesday to Thursday.

**Read the shape of a drop, not just its level** [dated, Sep 2026]. A campaign that's always been weak points at the offer or the list. A campaign that suddenly collapses points at something mechanical: a contaminated source, a sending-reputation event, or repeated/stale content. Monitor both a 7-day and a 14-day view and size the alert to the size of the decline, not to a single fixed threshold. Hold this diagnostic with appropriate looseness, not certainty: the claim that a sharp decline is "almost always" mechanical is stated with more confidence than the evidence for it, since mix, seasonality, and volume changes can also move a rate quickly, and a real incident investigation still means checking bounces and sends alongside everything else, which is a different job from tracking bounce rate as an ongoing north star (see the "Ignore" note above).

## 10. Lead magnets (his 2026 prediction)

- The big shift: AI-generated lead magnets, easy-yes offers, become the game-changer, above personalization. The industrialized form of bucket (3) is the Dream 100 at AI scale, section 13.
- **Three buckets:** (1) **knowledge** (an intelligence report: "I found companies with these 3 signals, want the report?"), (2) **micro-app** (spin up a small free tool in a weekend), (3) **agentic free trial** (run part of your service for free as the first touch).
- **Test the demand before building the magnet.** Send small campaigns for several magnet ideas, see which people want, then build only the winner. Don't build first.
- **Deliver cheap, then spend.** Send a cheap teaser analysis for a fraction of a cent; only spend real money building the full report for the people who positively respond.
- **Note the direction of value flow, since section 2 now has an offer archetype that runs the other way.** Every bucket here gives the prospect something free. The reversed-pitch, pay-for-their-data archetype in section 2 is deliberately not filed here, since it pays the prospect instead of giving them value; keep the two families separate rather than blending them.

## 11. AI and architecture (keep the principle, drop the tool)

- **Division of labor.** Do everything cheap, free, scalable, and non-prospect-facing in the agent/code layer (pull lists, scrape, enrich with open-source libraries and small local models, cache emails, store in a database). Do the prospect-facing final mile through a layer with real observability and human QA. His reason: he refuses to let a vibe-coded, unreviewed line reach a prospect, because one missed edge case sends crazy emails at scale.
- **The agnostic takeaway: keep a human-QA'd observability layer between the AI and the prospect.** The specific tool doesn't matter.
- **Cache every validated email in your own database; revalidate anything older than ~90 days.** Build a waterfall: cache first, then a cheap finder, then a validated finder, then send-data. Permutation-guess the easy emails before paying anyone.
- **Local open-source models on rented GPUs** for very high-volume cheap processing (only matters at his 8M/month scale).
- **The auto-research loop** (inspired by Karpathy's auto-research idea): read all past campaign results, sort winners from losers, propose the next round of experiments, rewrite copy, change list filters, relaunch. The CTA stays locked so it can never wander (e.g. give the product away). Runs weekly, human approves each relaunch. Doubled reply rates in an enterprise pilot with nobody writing copy by hand. This is the endgame: an AI running more experiments than a human ever could, compounding per client.
- **Pull the whole TAM up front precisely so the loop never makes game-time decisions.**
- **Bounded operations agents, with the approval line drawn in a specific place** [dated, Sep 2026, the newest and most concrete addition to this section]. Beyond the copy/experiment loop above, he now runs and describes several narrower agents doing bounded, scripted jobs against his own infrastructure and replies, not just campaign iteration: an agent that handles a fixed reply-scheduling script for positive responses (check booking status, offer times plus a calendar link, follow up if the times lapse, add context later); a scheduled agent that checks lead inventory, daily send volume, and inbox health, refills workflows automatically, and flags weak-performing domains for cancellation. **In every one of these, the pattern is the same and it is the load-bearing detail:** read-only monitoring and routine refills run unattended; anything consequential (cancelling infrastructure, a purchase, a send-volume change, a workflow rewrite) waits for him to review and act on it personally. He describes this explicitly as giving an agent scoped, enumerated tool access plus repeatable-check instructions, and requiring it to recommend before it acts, not "not autonomous magic." He's also praised, as a third party's design (not his own build), a similar shape at a more general productivity level: an agent that prepares work and presents it as a decision the user approves, rather than one that just acts.
- **The same approval discipline applies to agent-built workflows, not just ongoing ops agents** [dated, Sep 2026]. He dictates entire Clay enrichment workflows by voice to an agent rather than assembling them by hand, and the design detail he calls out explicitly is: build in fail-closed conditional stops (halt cleanly on a missing or invalid result rather than letting it fall through), merge validation results deliberately rather than automatically, and give the agent a pre-selected list of providers to use rather than letting it improvise its own sourcing. The agent executes a spec; it doesn't get to freelance the data sources.
- **Keep durable business context in files the company controls, decoupled from whichever agent or vendor executes against it** [dated, Sep 2026]. This is a distinct architectural point from the division-of-labor bullet above, not a restatement of it: company facts, the offer, SOPs, and tool needs belong in a portable, vendor-neutral store, precisely so switching agent tooling later doesn't mean rebuilding the company's own institutional memory from scratch.
- **Package multi-provider discovery and enrichment as a reusable, versioned skill, not a one-off script** [dated, Aug-Sep 2026]. The same discipline that produced the email-finding waterfall above (cache, then a cheap finder, then a validated finder) now extends to company and contact discovery generally: define the target and seed accounts, expand across several specialized providers, then find and verify, with the whole sequence held in one maintained, reusable orchestration unit rather than rebuilt per campaign. He maintains 20-plus such standardized lookups in his own stack as of a dated Sep 2026 demo. **Before swapping which providers sit inside that orchestration, benchmark them per section 15** rather than picking on reputation alone.
- **His own preference for which AI model writes better copy is not, by his own framing, evidence of anything yet** [dated, Sep 2026]. He ran the same campaign-strategy-and-copy request across several models and reported a subjective ranking (some outputs usable without edits, one not), while explicitly noting no reply, conversion, or cost data backs the ranking, and that he intends to test real results later. Treat this the same way he treats his own hedged numbers elsewhere: a live, public test-in-progress, not a settled position on model choice.

## 13. The Dream 100 at AI scale

- **The Dream 100 is Chet Holmes' (credit: *The Ultimate Sales Machine*): list your 100 dream customers, then do whatever it takes to earn their attention.** Create free value, reach multiple decision-makers, multi-thread. Eric's move is that the constraint on "whatever it takes" was always human hours, and AI removes it, so the list scales to a Dream 1,000 or a Dream 10,000. "We can use AI to make phenomenal lead magnets and show people the work that we've already done."
- **A job posting is not a trigger, it is a work order.** This is the mechanic and it's the sharpest idea in the play. A company hiring for a role has published a detailed, first-party, public spec of work it wants done badly enough to pay a salary for. So don't reference it. Do it. "Let's read the job description of what this person is going to be responsible for, and let's do a little bit of that with the APIs and the skills that are already available and made on my computer." Then the email is "I saw you're hiring for this person. You might not need to hire them, because we could do it for you. Here's what the output looks like."
- **Show the work you've already done, not a description of the work you could do.** His demo: a company hiring a growth lead for an ABM motion got a built ABM demo, with the target accounts, why each fits, the likely use case, the trigger signals to watch, the recommended personas, and per-account briefs. He built it from their job description, unprompted, before any reply.
- **Frame the artifact as a work sample, not an analysis.** "This is what the output would look like if you were to work with somebody like us." This is doing more work than it looks like it's doing: a sample is allowed to be approximate, and an analysis is not. It sets the reader's expectation to "here's how they work" instead of "here are facts about my business," which is the only framing that survives an artifact built entirely from the outside.
- **He shows the artifact and never a reply rate.** Read this section with that in mind. His stated evidence is "I actually don't even know how Codex did this. This was ridiculously smart," which is a demo of the build, not of the response. He also picks the friendliest possible case: a company hiring for exactly the thing he sells. The play is strongest where the job description *is* your service, and unproven everywhere else. **A dated, larger-scale repeat of the same pattern** [Sep 2026]: roughly 21,000 automated per-prospect audits for one customer, built with an AI browsing agent plus a language model, claimed to match manual audit quality, again with no reply rate, sampled quality check, or cost figure offered alongside the production count. Worth carrying a real engineering counterpoint alongside this one, surfaced when this claim was checked against primary sources: an API is often simpler and more reliable than browsing a page when the data you need is already available in structured form, a direct challenge to the assumption that browsing everything with an agent is automatically the right tool for scale.
- **The research half is now the easy half.** "The hardest part is writing the message, and that is going to take the most time for you to do the investment in order to fully automate that. But until you get that built, the easy part is the research." His whole framing: use AI to take research off your plate entirely, and spend the scarce effort on what you actually send. This is the same correction he made in section 4 when he demoted personalization, arriving from the other direction.

## 15. Vendor and provider benchmarking (test before you pay)

This section did not exist before the 2026-09-18 refresh. It is the single most corroborated new pattern across the entire batch of September material: independently, across list-building providers, tech-stack detection, email validators, and even AI model choice for research prompts, Eric applies the same discipline before committing spend or trust to a vendor. It is a direct, constructive extension of his own "marketing is a test-learn game" belief in section 0, and it fills in the positive half of the tell section 12 already records negatively ("pushes back on comparing yourself to someone else's benchmark").

- **The method, stated once and applied everywhere below.** Build a small, frozen, dated reference sample from a source you're actually authorized to access (his own recent positive replies, a manually checked set of companies, a known-good address list). Run every candidate vendor or method against that exact same sample. Then measure two things, not one: raw coverage or accuracy is close to worthless on its own, what matters is each additional vendor's **incremental, unique correct contribution** over what you already have, and its **false-negative rate** (how often it wrongly rejects something you already know is good). A vendor that looks weak on a general benchmark can still be the right addition if it recovers exactly the records your current stack misses.
- **Applied to list-building and contact-discovery providers.** When comparing routes to the same population (structured provider lookups, AI-native web search, direct website extraction, or several people-search APIs at once), score each on owner/contact accuracy, usable coverage, false positives, unique finds, cost, and time against the same manually verified answer key, then take the deduplicated union rather than picking one winner. This is exactly how he justified benchmarking Sales Navigator's own coverage against niche-specific providers before assuming it's automatically the best source for every buyer (section 3's Sales Navigator caveat is this method in action).
- **Applied to email validators.** Take a sample of addresses you *know* are live, because they recently produced real replies, and run it through each candidate validator. A validator that flags a meaningful share of that known-good sample as catch-all or invalid is silently costing you real yield (see section 3's yield caveat); the fix is swapping to whichever validator has the lower false-negative rate on your own data, not trusting either vendor's stated accuracy.
- **Applied to tech-stack and other detection providers.** The same "measure incremental unique contribution, not raw coverage" logic applies before adding a second or third detection provider on top of your primary one.
- **Applied to AI model and prompt choice for research work.** Test a cheaper research prompt or a different model against a reviewed answer key you already trust before swapping it in, the same discipline as vendor benchmarking, just applied to a prompt instead of a company.
- **The failure mode this discipline exists to prevent, in his own words about a related claim:** don't buy every available provider "just because an agency with many customers can justify them." More vendors stacked without this test just means more cost and more places for stale or wrong data to enter, not more coverage.

## 16. Infrastructure resilience and domain lifecycle

This section did not exist before the 2026-09-18 refresh. Section 1 has always called deliverability "a precondition, not a lever" and stopped there; this section is the operating rhythm he actually described for maintaining that precondition day to day, corroborated across several dated 2026 sources rather than resting on any single clip.

- **Hold reserve capacity ahead of need, not in response to a crisis.** Paid spare inbox capacity and spare domains, bought before you need them, so a health check that flags a weak domain has somewhere to reallocate sending volume immediately rather than waiting on new provisioning.
- **Run a recurring, multi-signal health check, not a single dashboard number.** His own described rhythm: daily spam checks, weekly reply-rate reviews, seed tests, and forwarding tests, alongside the ordinary list and offer work. No single one of these is trustworthy alone, which is consistent with section 9's instruction to ignore any one of them as a campaign-performance metric; used together as an infrastructure diagnostic, the same instruments answer a different, legitimate question (is the sending layer healthy) rather than the one section 9 says to stop asking of them (did this campaign perform well).
- **Have a written rule for when a domain or inbox gets retired or paused, and follow it on a schedule.** His own concrete instance: a weekly review, typically Friday, that cancels domains reply-rating under roughly 1%. **Hold this specific rule with real caution, not as a settled best practice**, for a reason he doesn't himself resolve: this is a domain-level action taken in response to a reply-rate shortfall, and elsewhere in this same corpus (sections 1 and 9) he's clear that a reply-rate shortfall more often points at the offer or the list than at the sending domain. He never actually claims, in describing this cutoff, that the domain itself was at fault rather than the list or offer behind it; treat the 1%-cutoff-and-cancel action as something he does, not as a diagnosis he's made, and keep the "check the offer and list first" instinct from section 1 firmly in view before recommending this to an operator as a root-cause fix rather than a capacity-management habit.
- **When reallocating capacity toward an apparently-stronger domain, the anti-gaming caution has to travel with the tactic, not get dropped.** Concentrating more inboxes on a domain that looks like a winner (he describes going as high as five inboxes on a single domain, versus a two-inbox baseline) contaminates the very comparison used to call it a winner in the first place; that domain may simply be sitting on an easier list or offer. Require real volume, a genuinely comparable assignment, and a full reply-lag window before ranking domains against each other, and revisit the ranking once you've changed what's sending through them.
- **Compliance is the floor under all of this, not a separate topic.** Regardless of vendor or domain strategy, commercial email still needs accurate sender identity, a working opt-out, and authentication practices that meet the sending platform's own bulk-sender rules; an agency sending on a client's behalf does not remove the client's own compliance obligation. This isn't a novel Eric position so much as a baseline that has to sit underneath every tactic in this section, and it's the reason the spintax compliance line in section 4 (never fake a title, never weaken the opt-out) belongs with deliverability tactics generally, not just with spintax specifically.
