# Plain Buyer Writing — Style and Standards

Writing that sounds smart is not always writing that works. Long sentences, technical words, and complex structure create distance between the writer and the reader. The goal is zero distance. The reader should feel like someone who understands their world wrote this — not someone selling to it.

This skill applies to every piece of written content Luke produces or asks Claude to produce.

---

## STEP 1 — UNDERSTAND THE BUYER FIRST

Before writing a single word, answer these three questions from what you already know about the target reader:

**1. What is their job title and daily world?**
What do they actually do all day? What words do they use at work? What problems show up in their inbox every morning? For Luke's buyers this means: staffing offices, vendor lists, bill rates, job orders, temp workers, placements, shifts, travel nurses, MSP contracts, and client accounts.

**2. What level of English do they read comfortably?**
Default assumption: Grade 5 to Grade 7 reading level. Short words. Short sentences. No jargon unless the buyer uses it themselves in their own job. When in doubt, use the simpler word.

**3. What do they already believe before they read this?**
Meet them where they are. Do not explain things they already know. Do not use words they would never say out loud. Do not write in a way that signals "I am a marketer" before they even finish the first line.

If you do not know the buyer clearly enough to answer these three questions, stop and check the reader-intelligence skill before writing.

## STEP 2 — APPLY THE PLAIN WRITING RULES

These rules apply to every sentence. No exceptions.

### Rule 1 — Use the simplest word that still means the same thing

| Instead of this | Write this |
|---|---|
| leverage | use |
| utilize | use |
| implement | set up / run / do |
| facilitate | help |
| generate pipeline | win new clients |
| outreach cadence | emails and messages |
| decision maker | the person who signs off |
| value proposition | why it works |
| done-for-you | we handle everything / you don't touch it |
| two-channel outreach | cold email and LinkedIn DM |
| deliverability | whether your emails land in the inbox |
| A/B testing | testing what works |
| ICP | the right type of client |
| TAM | total market size |

If the buyer would not say the word in a normal sentence at work, do not use it.

### Rule 2 — Write short sentences

One idea per sentence. If a sentence has more than 20 words, split it. Read every sentence out loud. If it sounds like something a real person would say in conversation, keep it. If it sounds like a brochure, rewrite it.

### Rule 3 — No filler, no padding

Cut any sentence that does not move the reader forward. Common filler to delete immediately:

- "In today's competitive landscape..."
- "As a leader in your space..."
- "We understand that your time is valuable..."
- "At the end of the day..."
- "That's where we come in."
- Any sentence that could apply to any company in any industry

### Rule 4 — Use second person

Write "you" not "staffing firms" or "our clients." The reader should feel like this was written for them personally, not for a category they belong to.

### Rule 5 — Name specific things

Vague writing loses trust. Specific writing builds it.

| Vague | Specific |
|---|---|
| "helps you win more business" | "books meetings with Plant Managers and VP Ops" |
| "improves your results" | "turned one closed account into 20x return on outreach spend" |
| "targets the right people" | "VP Operations, Plant Managers, CNOs, IT Directors" |
| "your BD efforts are inconsistent" | "cold calling runs all week and gets one meeting, maybe two" |

If you cannot be specific, ask Luke for the specific detail before writing. Do not fill gaps with vague language.

### Rule 6 — No em dashes

Never use em dashes. Use a comma, a period, or rewrite the sentence.

### Rule 7 — No marketing language in buyer-facing copy

These words and phrases are banned in anything written for Luke's buyers to read:

- pipeline (use "new business" or "new account flow")
- BD rep (use "the person handling sales" or rephrase)
- ICP (use "the right type of client" or just describe them)
- TOFU, MOFU, BOFU
- lead generation (use "winning new clients")
- value prop
- GTM
- outbound motion
- cadence (use "sequence" or "emails")
- done-for-you (use "we handle everything")
- two-channel (just name the channels)
- deliverability (explain it if needed)
- A/B testing (use "testing what works")

## STEP 3 — FORMAT FOR SKIMMABILITY

Most buyers skim before they read. Structure the content so the key message lands even on a 5-second scan.

**Use these formatting tools:**

- Short paragraphs (2 to 4 lines max)
- ↳ Arrow lists for problems or symptoms the reader recognizes
- ❌ Lists for negative outcomes or current failures
- 🎯 Lists for results and wins
- Numbered lists for steps or features
- Bold for the one thing in a section that must land

**Do not use:**

- Long paragraphs that bury the point
- Headers that are just marketing slogans
- Bullet points that repeat the same idea five different ways
- Emoji as decoration — only use them to organize or signal a section type

---

## STEP 4 — READ IT BACK AS THE BUYER

Before finalizing any piece of writing, ask this question:

"If a Plant Manager, VP Ops, CNO, or IT Director read this cold — no context, no relationship with Luke — would they recognize their own world in it?"

If the answer is yes, the writing is working.

If any sentence sounds like it was written by a marketer selling to a staffing firm instead of someone who has sat inside one, rewrite that sentence.

---

## QUICK SELF-CHECK BEFORE SUBMITTING

Run through this checklist on every piece of writing before handing it to Luke:

- [ ] No word on the banned list appears anywhere
- [ ] Every sentence is under 20 words or flows naturally if longer
- [ ] No sentence could apply to any other industry without changing it
- [ ] The buyer's actual world (shifts, job orders, bill rates, placements, vendor lists) is referenced at least once
- [ ] Specific numbers or examples are used instead of vague claims
- [ ] Reading it out loud sounds like a real person talking, not a brochure
- [ ] The formatting lets a skimmer get the main point in 5 seconds
